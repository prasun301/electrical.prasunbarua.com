DC Circuit Analysis: Series and Parallel Circuits Explained

Learn how to analyze DC electrical circuits using series and parallel resistance, voltage division, current division, Ohm's Law, and Kirchhoff's Laws with practical worked examples.

By Prasun Barua • September 23, 2026 • 12 min read

Understanding how to analyze a DC electrical circuit is one of the most important skills in electrical engineering. Once you understand voltage, current, resistance, Ohm's Law, and Kirchhoff's Laws, you can begin analyzing practical circuits containing multiple resistors and electrical sources.

In this article, we will learn how to analyze basic DC resistive circuits, including series circuits, parallel circuits, series-parallel circuits, voltage division, current division, and equivalent resistance.

What Is DC Circuit Analysis?

DC circuit analysis is the process of determining electrical quantities such as voltage, current, resistance, and power in a direct-current circuit.

In a DC circuit:

  • Voltage has a fixed polarity.
  • Current flows in a defined direction.
  • Resistance determines the relationship between voltage and current.
  • Ohm's Law can be used for individual resistive elements.
  • Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) can be used for more complex circuits.

The fundamental relationship for a resistor is:

V = IR

Where:

  • V = voltage across the resistor in volts (V)
  • I = current through the resistor in amperes (A)
  • R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

Basic Steps for Analyzing a DC Circuit

A systematic approach makes circuit analysis much easier.

Step 1: Identify the Circuit Components

Identify:

  • DC voltage sources
  • Resistors
  • Switches
  • Circuit branches
  • Nodes
  • Series connections
  • Parallel connections

Step 2: Determine the Circuit Configuration

Determine whether the resistors are connected in series, in parallel, or in a combination of series and parallel.

Step 3: Calculate Equivalent Resistance

Simplify the circuit by replacing groups of resistors with an equivalent resistance.

Step 4: Calculate Total Current

Once the equivalent resistance is known, use Ohm's Law:

IT = VT / RT

Step 5: Determine Individual Voltages and Currents

Use Ohm's Law, voltage divider, current divider, KCL, and KVL as appropriate.

Step 6: Check the Result

A good circuit analysis should satisfy KCL at nodes, KVL around closed loops, Ohm's Law for resistive elements, and power balance.

Series DC Circuits

Two or more resistors are connected in series when they are connected end-to-end and the same current flows through every resistor.

In a series circuit, there is only one path for current.

Current in a Series Circuit

The current is the same through every resistor:

IT = I1 = I2 = I3

Equivalent Resistance of Series Resistors

For resistors connected in series:

RT = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

Therefore, the total resistance is simply the sum of all resistances.

Example: Series Circuit

Suppose a 24 V DC source supplies three resistors:

  • R1 = 4 Ω
  • R2 = 6 Ω
  • R3 = 10 Ω

First calculate the total resistance:

RT = 4 + 6 + 10
RT = 20 Ω

Now calculate the total current:

IT = 24 / 20
IT = 1.2 A

Because the resistors are in series:

I1 = I2 = I3 = 1.2 A

Voltage across R1:

V1 = IR1
V1 = 1.2 × 4
V1 = 4.8 V

Voltage across R2:

V2 = 1.2 × 6
V2 = 7.2 V

Voltage across R3:

V3 = 1.2 × 10
V3 = 12 V

Check using KVL:

24 − 4.8 − 7.2 − 12 = 0

Therefore, the result satisfies Kirchhoff's Voltage Law.

Voltage Divider Rule

The voltage divider rule is useful for finding the voltage across an individual resistor in a series circuit.

For two series resistors:

V1 = VT × R1 / (R1 + R2)

Similarly:

V2 = VT × R2 / (R1 + R2)

The resistor with the larger resistance receives the larger voltage drop.

Example

A 12 V source is connected to:

  • R1 = 2 kΩ
  • R2 = 4 kΩ

Voltage across R2:

V2 = 12 × 4 / (2 + 4)
V2 = 8 V

Voltage across R1:

V1 = 12 × 2 / (2 + 4)
V1 = 4 V

Check:

V1 + V2 = 4 + 8 = 12 V

Parallel DC Circuits

Two or more resistors are connected in parallel when their terminals are connected to the same two nodes.

In a parallel circuit, every branch has the same voltage.

VT = V1 = V2 = V3

However, the current through each branch may be different.

Equivalent Resistance of Two Parallel Resistors

For two resistors in parallel:

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)

Example

Suppose:

  • R1 = 6 Ω
  • R2 = 3 Ω
  • V = 12 V

The equivalent resistance is:

RT = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3)
RT = 18 / 9
RT = 2 Ω

The total current is:

IT = 12 / 2
IT = 6 A

Current through R1:

I1 = 12 / 6
I1 = 2 A

Current through R2:

I2 = 12 / 3
I2 = 4 A

Using KCL:

IT = I1 + I2
IT = 2 + 4
IT = 6 A

The result agrees with the total current calculated using the equivalent resistance.

Parallel Resistance for Multiple Resistors

For three or more resistors in parallel:

1 / RT = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3 + ...

Therefore:

RT = 1 / (1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...)

An important property of parallel resistors is:

The equivalent resistance of parallel resistors is always less than the smallest individual resistance.

Current Divider Rule

The current divider rule can be used to determine how current divides between parallel resistors.

For two parallel resistors:

I1 = IT × R2 / (R1 + R2)

And:

I2 = IT × R1 / (R1 + R2)

Notice an important principle:

Current divides inversely according to resistance.

The branch with lower resistance carries more current.

Series-Parallel DC Circuits

Many practical circuits contain both series and parallel connections. These are called series-parallel circuits.

The correct approach is to simplify the circuit step by step.

Example

Suppose:

  • R1 = 4 Ω
  • R2 = 6 Ω
  • R3 = 3 Ω
  • V = 24 V

R2 and R3 are parallel.

First calculate their equivalent resistance:

R23 = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3)
R23 = 18 / 9
R23 = 2 Ω

Now R1 and R23 are in series:

RT = 4 + 2
RT = 6 Ω

Total current:

IT = 24 / 6
IT = 4 A

Voltage across R1:

V1 = 4 × 4
V1 = 16 V

Therefore, voltage across the parallel combination is:

V23 = 24 − 16
V23 = 8 V

Since R2 and R3 are parallel:

V2 = V3 = 8 V

Current through R2:

I2 = 8 / 6
I2 = 1.33 A

Current through R3:

I3 = 8 / 3
I3 = 2.67 A

Check using KCL:

IT = I2 + I3
4 = 1.33 + 2.67
4 = 4 A

The result is consistent.

Using Kirchhoff's Laws in DC Circuit Analysis

Not every circuit can be simplified using only series and parallel resistance formulas.

For more complicated circuits, Kirchhoff's Laws become essential.

Kirchhoff's Current Law

KCL states that the total current entering a node equals the total current leaving the node.

ΣIin = ΣIout

This is particularly useful for circuits containing multiple branches.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

KVL states that the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.

ΣV = 0

It can also be written as:

ΣVrise = ΣVdrop
Total voltage rises equal total voltage drops around a closed loop

KCL and KVL provide the foundation for more advanced circuit-analysis techniques such as mesh analysis and nodal analysis.

Power in a DC Circuit

Once voltage and current are known, electrical power can be calculated.

The basic power equation is:

P = VI

For a resistor, Ohm's Law allows two additional forms:

P = I2R
P = V2 / R

Where:

  • P = power in watts (W)
  • V = voltage in volts (V)
  • I = current in amperes (A)
  • R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

Example

A 10 Ω resistor carries 2 A.

Power dissipated:

P = I2R
P = 22 × 10
P = 40 W

Therefore, the resistor dissipates 40 W.

In a complete circuit, the power supplied by the source should equal the total power consumed by the circuit, assuming ideal components.

DC Circuit Analysis: A Practical Method

For a practical circuit, the following procedure is useful.

1. Draw the Circuit Clearly

Identify all sources, resistors, nodes, and branches.

2. Label Known Values

Write the known voltage, resistance, and current values.

3. Identify Series and Parallel Combinations

Look for resistors that can be combined.

4. Calculate Equivalent Resistance

Reduce the circuit step by step.

5. Calculate Total Current

IT = VT / RT

6. Work Backward Through the Circuit

Determine individual voltages and currents.

7. Apply KCL and KVL Where Necessary

Use Kirchhoff's Laws when the circuit cannot be fully simplified.

8. Calculate Power

Determine the power consumed by each important component.

9. Verify the Result

Check:

  • Current balance
  • Voltage balance
  • Resistance calculations
  • Power balance

Common Mistakes in DC Circuit Analysis

Mistake 1: Assuming Current Is the Same Everywhere

Current is the same through series components, but not necessarily through parallel branches.

Mistake 2: Assuming Voltage Is the Same Everywhere

Voltage is the same across components connected in parallel, but not generally across series components.

Mistake 3: Adding Parallel Resistances Directly

For parallel resistors, do not use:

RT = R1 + R2

That formula applies to series resistors.

Mistake 4: Forgetting Units

Always identify whether resistance is in Ω, kΩ, or MΩ and whether current is in A or mA.

Mistake 5: Not Checking the Result

Use KCL, KVL, or power balance to verify the calculated values.

Series vs Parallel Circuits

Property Series Circuit Parallel Circuit
Current Same through all components Divides between branches
Voltage Divides between components Same across branches
Equivalent resistance Sum of resistances Less than the smallest resistance
Number of current paths One Multiple
Main analysis rule KVL / Ohm's Law KCL / Ohm's Law

Why DC Circuit Analysis Is Important

DC circuit analysis is fundamental to electrical engineering because it provides the basis for understanding more advanced electrical systems.

The same concepts are used when analyzing:

  • Battery-powered systems
  • Control circuits
  • Electronic circuits
  • DC power supplies
  • Solar PV systems
  • Battery energy storage systems
  • Electrical protection circuits
  • Instrumentation circuits
  • Industrial control systems

For solar PV engineers, understanding DC circuits is particularly important because photovoltaic modules, strings, combiner boxes, and many battery systems operate on the DC side of an electrical installation.

Summary

DC circuit analysis involves determining the voltage, current, resistance, and power of components within a DC electrical circuit.

The most important principles are:

  1. Series resistors have the same current.
  2. Parallel resistors have the same voltage.
  3. Series resistances are added directly.
  4. Parallel resistance is calculated using reciprocal relationships.
  5. Voltage divides across series resistors.
  6. Current divides between parallel branches.
  7. KCL is used for current relationships at nodes.
  8. KVL is used for voltage relationships around closed loops.
  9. Ohm's Law connects voltage, current, and resistance.
  10. Power can be calculated using P = VI, I²R, or V²/R.

Once these principles are understood, you are ready to study more advanced circuit-analysis techniques such as mesh analysis and nodal analysis.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is DC circuit analysis?

DC circuit analysis is the process of determining voltage, current, resistance, and power in a direct-current electrical circuit.

What is the difference between series and parallel circuits?

In a series circuit, the same current flows through each component. In a parallel circuit, the voltage across each branch is the same while current divides between the branches.

What is the voltage divider rule?

The voltage divider rule calculates the voltage across a resistor in a series circuit based on its resistance relative to the total resistance.

What is the current divider rule?

The current divider rule calculates how total current divides between parallel branches. A lower-resistance branch carries more current.

When should Kirchhoff's Laws be used?

Kirchhoff's Laws are particularly useful when a circuit cannot be completely simplified using basic series and parallel resistance formulas.

What is equivalent resistance?

Equivalent resistance is the single resistance value that represents a group of resistors while producing the same overall voltage-current relationship at the circuit terminals.
