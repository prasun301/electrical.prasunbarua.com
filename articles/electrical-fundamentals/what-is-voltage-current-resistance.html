What Are Voltage, Current and Resistance?

Voltage, current and resistance are three of the most fundamental quantities in electrical engineering. Understanding how they work together is essential for analyzing electrical circuits.

By Prasun Barua • August 12, 2026 • Electrical Fundamentals

To understand electrical engineering, it is important to understand three basic quantities: voltage, current and resistance. These quantities appear in almost every electrical circuit and are closely related to each other.

Their relationship is described by Ohm's Law, which is one of the fundamental principles used for electrical circuit analysis.

What Is Voltage?

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points in a circuit. It provides the electrical potential that can drive current through a circuit.

Voltage is measured in volts (V).

A useful way to understand voltage is to compare it with pressure in a water system. Water pressure can provide the force that moves water through a pipe. Similarly, electrical voltage provides the potential difference that can cause electric charge to move.

Voltage = V
Unit: volt (V)

Examples of Voltage

Common electrical voltage levels include:

  • A typical AA battery: approximately 1.5 V
  • A common USB supply: approximately 5 V
  • A typical 12 V automotive battery system: approximately 12 V
  • Residential and commercial AC systems use higher voltages depending on the country and electrical system.

What Is Electric Current?

Electric current is the rate at which electric charge flows through a conductor or electrical circuit.

Current is measured in amperes (A), commonly shortened to amps.

In a simple water analogy, current can be compared with the amount of water flowing through a pipe per unit of time.

Current = I
Unit: ampere (A)

Conventional Current

In conventional electrical circuit analysis, current is considered to flow from a higher electrical potential toward a lower electrical potential through the external circuit.

The physical movement of electrons in metallic conductors is in the opposite direction to conventional current.

This distinction is important when studying electrical circuits and electronic systems.

What Is Electrical Resistance?

Resistance is the opposition offered by a material or component to the flow of electric current.

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

In the water analogy, resistance can be compared with a restriction or narrow section of a pipe that makes it more difficult for water to flow.

Resistance = R
Unit: ohm (Ω)

Factors Affecting Resistance

The resistance of a conductor depends on several factors, including:

  • Material of the conductor
  • Length of the conductor
  • Cross-sectional area
  • Temperature

For a uniform conductor, resistance can be expressed as:

R = ρL ÷ A
Resistance = resistivity × length ÷ cross-sectional area

How Voltage, Current and Resistance Are Related

Voltage, current and resistance are related through Ohm's Law.

V = I × R
Voltage = Current × Resistance

This equation tells us that, for an ohmic component under appropriate conditions, increasing voltage while resistance remains constant results in increased current.

Similarly, increasing resistance while voltage remains constant reduces the current.

The Three Basic Ohm's Law Equations

Calculate Voltage

V = I × R

Calculate Current

I = V ÷ R

Calculate Resistance

R = V ÷ I

Practical Example

Consider a simple DC circuit supplied by a 12 V source and connected to a 6 Ω resistor.

Given

  • Voltage = 12 V
  • Resistance = 6 Ω

We want to calculate the current.

Using Ohm's Law:

I = V ÷ R
I = 12 ÷ 6
I = 2 A

Therefore, the current through the resistor is 2 A, assuming the stated circuit conditions and an appropriate ohmic model.

Voltage, Current and Resistance Water Analogy

A water-system analogy can help beginners visualize the three electrical quantities.

Electrical Quantity Water Analogy Unit
Voltage Pressure difference Volt (V)
Current Water flow rate Ampere (A)
Resistance Restriction to flow Ohm (Ω)

The water analogy is useful for building intuition, but real electrical systems are more complex than a simple water-pipe model.

Voltage, Current and Resistance in a Series Circuit

In a basic series circuit, the same current flows through each series component.

The total resistance of resistors connected in series is:

RT = R1 + R2 + R3
Total resistance in a series circuit

The source voltage is distributed among the components according to their electrical characteristics.

Voltage, Current and Resistance in a Parallel Circuit

In an ideal parallel circuit, each branch is connected across the same two supply nodes, so the voltage across each branch is the same.

The total current is the sum of the branch currents:

IT = I1 + I2 + I3

For resistors connected in parallel:

1 ÷ RT = 1 ÷ R1 + 1 ÷ R2 + 1 ÷ R3

Relationship With Electrical Power

Voltage and current are also directly related to electrical power.

P = V × I
Electrical power in watts

Combining this equation with Ohm's Law gives:

P = I²R
P = V² ÷ R

These formulas are commonly used when analyzing electrical loads, resistive circuits and equipment power consumption.

Practical Electrical Engineering Applications

Understanding voltage, current and resistance is essential in many practical engineering tasks.

  • Electrical circuit analysis
  • Voltage-drop calculations
  • Cable sizing
  • Fuse and circuit-breaker selection
  • Resistor selection
  • Battery system analysis
  • DC system design
  • Solar PV system calculations
  • Electrical troubleshooting
  • Testing and commissioning

Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Confusing Voltage With Current

Voltage and current are different electrical quantities. Voltage is a potential difference, while current represents the rate of charge flow.

Confusing Current With Power

Current is measured in amperes, while electrical power is measured in watts. They are related, but they are not the same quantity.

Forgetting Units

Always check units when performing electrical calculations. Voltage should be expressed in volts, current in amperes and resistance in ohms when using the basic Ohm's Law equations directly.

Assuming Every Device Is Ohmic

Not every electrical device has a constant resistance. Components such as diodes and many semiconductor devices have nonlinear voltage-current characteristics.

Engineering note: Ohm's Law is a powerful circuit-analysis tool, but real equipment should always be evaluated using appropriate component characteristics, operating conditions, manufacturer specifications and applicable electrical standards.

Summary

Voltage, current and resistance are fundamental concepts in electrical engineering.

Quantity Symbol Unit Basic Meaning
Voltage V Volt (V) Electrical potential difference
Current I Ampere (A) Rate of electric charge flow
Resistance R Ohm (Ω) Opposition to current flow

Their fundamental relationship can be expressed using Ohm's Law:

V = I × R

Once these three concepts are understood, many other electrical engineering topics become much easier to analyze and understand.
