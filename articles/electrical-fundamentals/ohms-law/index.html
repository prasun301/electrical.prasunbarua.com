What Is Ohm's Law? Formula, Examples & Practical Guide

Ohm's Law describes the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in an electrical circuit. It is one of the most fundamental relationships used in electrical engineering.

By Prasun Barua • August 12, 2026 • Electrical Fundamentals

Ohm's Law is one of the most important concepts in electrical engineering. It provides a simple mathematical relationship between voltage, current, and resistance.

Understanding this relationship makes it easier to analyze electrical circuits, select components, perform calculations, and troubleshoot electrical systems.

What Is Ohm's Law?

Ohm's Law states that the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage applied across it, provided the physical conditions of the conductor remain approximately constant.

The basic mathematical relationship is:

V = I × R
Voltage = Current × Resistance

The three electrical quantities are:

  • V = Voltage, measured in volts (V)
  • I = Current, measured in amperes (A)
  • R = Resistance, measured in ohms (Ω)

Ohm's Law Formulas

By rearranging the basic equation, we can calculate any one of the three quantities when the other two are known.

V = I × R
Calculate voltage
I = V ÷ R
Calculate current
R = V ÷ I
Calculate resistance

Understanding Voltage, Current and Resistance

Voltage

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It provides the electrical potential that drives current through a circuit.

Voltage is measured in volts (V).

Current

Electric current represents the rate at which electric charge flows through a conductor.

Current is measured in amperes (A).

Resistance

Resistance is the opposition to the flow of electric current through a material or component.

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

Practical Example of Ohm's Law

Suppose a 12 V DC power supply is connected to a resistor having a resistance of 6 Ω.

Given:

  • Voltage = 12 V
  • Resistance = 6 Ω

We need to calculate the current.

Using the Ohm's Law current equation:

I = V ÷ R
I = 12 ÷ 6
I = 2 A

Therefore, the current flowing through the resistor is 2 A.

Another Example

Consider a circuit supplied by 24 V with a current of 3 A. What is the resistance?

R = V ÷ I
R = 24 ÷ 3
R = 8 Ω

Therefore, the resistance is 8 Ω.

Ohm's Law Calculation Table

Known Values Formula Quantity Calculated
Current + Resistance V = I × R Voltage
Voltage + Resistance I = V ÷ R Current
Voltage + Current R = V ÷ I Resistance

Ohm's Law and Electrical Power

Ohm's Law can also be combined with the electrical power equation to derive several useful formulas.

P = V × I
Electrical power

By substituting the Ohm's Law relationships, additional power formulas can be obtained.

P = I²R
P = V² ÷ R

These relationships are widely used in electrical circuit calculations and equipment design.

Applications of Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law is used in many areas of electrical and electronic engineering.

  • Electrical circuit analysis
  • Cable and conductor calculations
  • Voltage-drop calculations
  • Resistor selection
  • Power calculations
  • Electrical troubleshooting
  • Electronic circuit design
  • Basic solar PV calculations

Limitations of Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law is extremely useful, but it does not describe every electrical device under every operating condition.

Some components are non-ohmic, meaning their resistance or voltage-current relationship changes with voltage, current, temperature, or other operating conditions.

Examples include semiconductor devices such as diodes and many electronic components.

Engineering note: Always consider the actual characteristics, operating temperature, manufacturer data and applicable electrical standards when performing engineering design calculations.

Summary

Ohm's Law provides a fundamental relationship between voltage, current, and resistance.

V = I × R

From this equation, we can calculate voltage, current, or resistance when the other two values are known.

Because of its simplicity and wide range of applications, Ohm's Law is one of the first principles electrical engineering students and practicing engineers should understand.
