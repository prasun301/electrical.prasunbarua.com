Ohm's Law Explained: Formula, Examples & Calculations

Ohm's Law is one of the most foundational relationships in electrical engineering. It describes how voltage, current, and resistance interact within an electrical circuit.

Quick Answer

Ohm's Law states that the current ($I$) flowing through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage ($V$) across the two points and inversely proportional to the resistance ($R$).

V = I × R

What Is Ohm's Law?

Ohm's Law is a fundamental principle used in electrical and electronic engineering to calculate the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in an electrical circuit.

When the resistance of a circuit is constant, increasing the voltage results in a proportional increase in current. Conversely, if resistance increases for a given voltage, current decreases.

The three fundamental electrical quantities are:

  • Voltage (V) — Electrical potential difference, measured in Volts (V).
  • Current (I) — The flow rate of electric charge, measured in Amperes (A).
  • Resistance (R) — The opposition to current flow, measured in Ohms (Ω).

Ohm's Law Formula

The standard algebraic expression for Ohm's Law is:

V = I × R

Where:

Symbol Quantity Unit
V Voltage Volt (V)
I Current Ampere (A)
R Resistance Ohm (Ω)

Ohm's Law Triangle

The formula can be rearranged depending on which quantity needs to be calculated:

Voltage V = I × R
Current I = V ÷ R
Resistance R = V ÷ I

Worked Example

Suppose a 24 V DC supply is connected across a resistor with a resistance of 12 Ω. What current will flow through the circuit?

Step 1 — Identify the known values

Voltage ($V$) = 24 V

Resistance ($R$) = 12 Ω

Step 2 — Apply Ohm's Law for current

I = V ÷ R

Step 3 — Substitute values and calculate

I = 24 ÷ 12
I = 2 A

Therefore, the total current flowing through the resistor is 2 amperes.

Engineering Notes

While Ohm's Law is the baseline for simple linear DC circuits, practical AC power networks involve frequency-dependent components like inductors and capacitors.

In AC circuits, pure resistance ($R$) is expanded to total impedance ($Z$), modifying the relationship to $V = I \times Z$. Furthermore, Ohm's Law is frequently combined with Joule's Law to determine electrical power dissipation:

P = V × I = I² × R

Combining these equations enables power system engineers to calculate power loss, voltage drop across distribution lines, and fault current magnitudes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic formula of Ohm's Law?

The basic formula is $V = I \times R$, where $V$ is voltage in volts, $I$ is current in amperes, and $R$ is resistance in ohms.

What happens when resistance increases?

If voltage remains constant, increasing the circuit resistance reduces current flow proportionally according to $I = V \div R$.

Is Ohm's Law applicable to AC circuits?

Ohm's Law applies directly to resistive AC loads. However, for loads with inductive or capacitive components, total impedance ($Z$) must be used instead of simple resistance ($R$).
