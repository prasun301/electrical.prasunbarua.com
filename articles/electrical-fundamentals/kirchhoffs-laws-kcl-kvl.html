Kirchhoff's Laws: KCL and KVL Explained with Examples

Learn Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL), understand their physical meaning and sign conventions, and solve practical electrical circuit problems using worked examples.

By Prasun Barua • Published August 12, 2026 • Updated September 13, 2026 • 12 min read

Kirchhoff's Laws are two of the most important principles used in electrical circuit analysis. They provide a systematic way to determine unknown currents and voltages in electrical networks.

The two laws are known as Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL). KCL deals with the currents entering and leaving a circuit node, while KVL deals with voltage rises and voltage drops around a closed circuit loop.

Together, these laws are based on fundamental conservation principles and form the foundation of many circuit-analysis techniques, including node analysis and mesh analysis.

Quick engineering answer: KCL states that the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero, while KVL states that the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.

What Are Kirchhoff's Laws?

Kirchhoff's Laws were formulated by German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and are fundamental tools for analyzing electrical networks.

The two laws are:

  • Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — applies to currents at a node.
  • Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) — applies to voltages around a closed loop.

KCL is based on conservation of electric charge, while KVL is based on conservation of energy.

∑ I = 0
Kirchhoff's Current Law
∑ V = 0
Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of all currents at a circuit node is zero.

∑ I = 0
Algebraic sum of currents at a node

Another commonly used form of KCL is:

∑ Iin = ∑ Iout
Total current entering a node equals total current leaving the node

This law follows from the principle that electric charge cannot accumulate indefinitely at an ideal circuit node.

Physical Basis of KCL

KCL is based on the conservation of electric charge. At an ideal node, charge does not continuously appear or disappear.

Therefore, the total current flowing into the node must equal the total current flowing out of the node.

For example, if two currents enter a node and two currents leave it:

I1 + I2 = I3 + I4

Rearranging gives the algebraic form:

I1 + I2 − I3 − I4 = 0

KCL Sign Convention

A consistent sign convention should be selected when applying KCL.

One common convention is:

  • Currents entering the node are positive.
  • Currents leaving the node are negative.

Another valid convention is to assign negative signs to entering currents and positive signs to leaving currents. The important point is to use the same convention throughout the calculation.

Engineering note: The choice of positive current direction is arbitrary. A negative calculated current simply means that the actual current direction is opposite to the assumed reference direction.

KCL Worked Example

Consider a node where two currents of 5 A and 3 A enter the node. A current of 4 A leaves the node. Determine the remaining current.

Given

  • I1 = 5 A entering
  • I2 = 3 A entering
  • I3 = 4 A leaving
  • I = unknown leaving current

Applying KCL:

5 + 3 = 4 + I

Therefore:

I = 8 − 4 I = 4 A

Therefore, the unknown current is 4 A leaving the node.

Checking KCL:

Total entering current = 5 + 3 = 8 A Total leaving current = 4 + 4 = 8 A

Therefore, the KCL equation is satisfied.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around any closed electrical loop is zero.

∑ V = 0
Algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop

KVL follows from the conservation of energy. When a charge travels around a complete closed loop and returns to its starting point, the net change in electrical potential energy is zero.

In practical circuit analysis, voltage sources are treated as voltage rises and circuit elements such as resistors are commonly treated as voltage drops in the direction of current.

KVL Sign Convention

A consistent sign convention is essential when applying KVL.

When moving around a loop:

  • Moving from the negative terminal of a voltage source to the positive terminal is a voltage rise.
  • Moving from the positive terminal to the negative terminal is a voltage drop.
  • Across a resistor, moving in the direction of current is normally treated as a voltage drop.
  • Moving through a resistor opposite to the assumed current direction is treated as a voltage rise.

The exact signs depend on the chosen loop direction and reference polarities.

KVL Worked Example

Consider a simple series circuit containing a 12 V source and two resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω.

Given

  • Supply voltage = 12 V
  • R1 = 2 Ω
  • R2 = 4 Ω

First calculate the total resistance:

RT = 2 + 4 RT = 6 Ω

The circuit current is:

I = V ÷ R I = 12 ÷ 6 I = 2 A

The voltage drop across the first resistor is:

V1 = I × R1 V1 = 2 × 2 V1 = 4 V

The voltage drop across the second resistor is:

V2 = I × R2 V2 = 2 × 4 V2 = 8 V

Now apply KVL around the complete loop:

+12 − (2 × 2) − (2 × 4) = 0

Therefore:

12 − 4 − 8 = 0

Therefore:

0 = 0 ✓

The KVL equation is satisfied.

KCL vs KVL

KCL and KVL describe different aspects of an electrical circuit, but they work together during circuit analysis.

Comparison of Kirchhoff's Current Law and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law
Feature KCL KVL
Full name Kirchhoff's Current Law Kirchhoff's Voltage Law
Applies to Nodes Closed loops
Main quantity Current Voltage
Fundamental principle Conservation of charge Conservation of energy
Basic equation ∑ I = 0 ∑ V = 0

Using KCL and KVL Together

More complex circuits often contain multiple nodes and multiple loops. In such circuits, KCL and KVL can be used together to create simultaneous equations.

For example, KCL can first be used to establish relationships between branch currents, while KVL can then be used to establish voltage relationships around independent loops.

The resulting equations can be solved simultaneously to determine unknown currents and voltages.

∑ I = 0
∑ V = 0

This combination is particularly useful for circuits containing several resistors, voltage sources and current sources.

Relationship to Mesh and Node Analysis

Kirchhoff's Laws form the theoretical foundation of two widely used circuit-analysis techniques: node analysis and mesh analysis.

Node Analysis

Node analysis primarily uses KCL. A reference node is selected and the voltages of other nodes are determined by applying current relationships.

Mesh Analysis

Mesh analysis primarily uses KVL. Loop or mesh currents are assigned and voltage equations are written around the independent loops.

Both methods are systematic applications of Kirchhoff's Laws and are especially useful when circuits become too complex for simple series and parallel reduction.

Practical Applications of Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's Laws are used throughout electrical and electronic engineering.

  • DC circuit analysis
  • AC circuit analysis
  • Electronic circuit design
  • Power supply analysis
  • Electrical network analysis
  • Node-voltage calculations
  • Mesh-current calculations
  • Fault and network analysis
  • Control and instrumentation circuits
  • Electrical engineering education and laboratory analysis

The laws remain useful whether the circuit is a simple battery-resistor network or a much more complex electrical network.

Common Mistakes When Applying KCL and KVL

Several mistakes can lead to incorrect circuit equations.

  • Using inconsistent current directions
  • Mixing voltage polarity conventions
  • Forgetting a branch current in a KCL equation
  • Forgetting a voltage source or voltage drop in a KVL equation
  • Incorrectly identifying nodes
  • Using an incorrect resistor voltage drop direction
  • Changing the sign convention in the middle of a calculation
  • Assuming a negative calculated current means the calculation is automatically wrong
Important: A negative current or voltage result is often perfectly valid. It usually means that the actual direction or polarity is opposite to the reference direction initially assumed.

Key Takeaways

  • KCL applies to currents at a node.
  • KVL applies to voltages around a closed loop.
  • KCL is based on conservation of electric charge.
  • KVL is based on conservation of energy.
  • The basic KCL relationship is ∑ I = 0.
  • The basic KVL relationship is ∑ V = 0.
  • A consistent sign convention should always be used.
  • Negative calculated values can indicate that the actual direction is opposite to the assumed reference direction.
  • KCL and KVL provide the foundation for node-voltage and mesh-current analysis.
