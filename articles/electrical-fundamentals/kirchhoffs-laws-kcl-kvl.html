Kirchhoff's Laws: KCL and KVL Explained with Examples

By Prasun Barua Published September 13, 2026 Updated September 13, 2026

Kirchhoff's Laws are two of the most important principles used to analyze electrical circuits. They provide a systematic way to determine unknown currents and voltages in circuits ranging from simple resistor networks to complex power and electronic systems.

The two laws are known as Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL). KCL is based on the conservation of electric charge, while KVL is based on the conservation of energy.

Quick answer

KCL: The total current entering a node equals the total current leaving the node.

KVL: The algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around a closed loop is zero.

What Are Kirchhoff's Laws?

German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff developed the circuit laws in the nineteenth century. Today, the laws are fundamental tools in electrical engineering because they remain valid for a broad range of lumped electrical circuits.

Together with Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's Laws allow engineers and students to solve circuits whose current and voltage relationships cannot be determined using a single resistor equation.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of all currents at a circuit node is zero.

∑ I = 0
Algebraic sum of currents at a node

An equivalent and often more intuitive form is:

Σ Iin = Σ Iout
Total current entering a node equals total current leaving it

Why Does KCL Work?

KCL follows from the principle of conservation of electric charge. Charge cannot accumulate indefinitely at an ideal circuit node, so the current flowing into the node must be balanced by the current flowing out.

KCL Sign Convention

When applying KCL, choose a current sign convention and use it consistently. A common convention is:

  • Currents entering the node are positive.
  • Currents leaving the node are negative.

The reverse convention is also valid. The important point is consistency.

KCL Worked Example

Suppose a node has two currents entering the node: 5 A and 3 A. One current of 4 A leaves the node, while the remaining current is unknown.

Iin = Iout

5 + 3 = 4 + I

8 = 4 + I

I = 4 A

Therefore, the unknown current leaving the node is 4 A.

Practical Meaning of KCL

In practical electrical networks, KCL is used whenever current splits between branches. It is particularly useful for parallel circuits, distribution networks, electronic circuits, power converters, and nodal analysis.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around any closed loop is zero.

∑ V = 0
Algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop

In other words, the total voltage rise supplied by sources must equal the total voltage drop across the circuit elements in a closed loop.

Why Does KVL Work?

KVL is based on conservation of energy. When a charge travels around a complete closed path and returns to its starting point, its net change in electrical potential energy must be zero.

KVL Sign Convention

A voltage source or component can be treated as a voltage rise or a voltage drop depending on the direction in which the loop is traversed.

  • Going from negative to positive across a source is commonly treated as a positive voltage rise.
  • Going from positive to negative across a source is commonly treated as a voltage drop.
  • Across a resistor in the direction of current, the voltage change is commonly treated as a drop.

KVL Worked Example

Consider a simple loop containing a 12 V source and two series resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω.

Step 1: Find the Total Resistance

RT = R1 + R2

RT = 2 + 4

RT = 6 Ω

Step 2: Calculate the Circuit Current

Using Ohm's law:

V = IR

I = V / R

I = 12 / 6

I = 2 A

Step 3: Apply KVL

Going around the loop, the source voltage must equal the sum of the resistor voltage drops:

+12 − (2 × 2) − (2 × 4) = 0

+12 − 4 − 8 = 0

0 = 0

The result confirms Kirchhoff's Voltage Law for the loop.

Using KCL and KVL Together

KCL and KVL are not competing methods. In many practical circuit problems they are used together.

For example, KCL can be used to establish the relationships between branch currents at circuit nodes, while KVL can be used to establish voltage relationships around loops.

With Ohm's law, these relationships can be converted into simultaneous equations and solved for the unknown circuit quantities.

V = IR
Ohm's law links voltage, current, and resistance

Relationship to Nodal Analysis

Nodal analysis primarily uses KCL. The voltage at each important circuit node is selected as an unknown, and current equations are written according to KCL.

The current in each branch can then be expressed using Ohm's law.

For example, if a resistor connects two nodes with voltages V1 and V2, its current can be written as:

I = (V1 − V2) / R

Relationship to Mesh Analysis

Mesh analysis primarily uses KVL. A current is assigned to each independent mesh, and voltage equations are written around each mesh.

The resulting simultaneous equations can be solved for the unknown mesh currents.

Practical Applications of Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's Laws are used throughout electrical and electronic engineering. Typical applications include:

  • Analysis of DC resistor circuits.
  • AC circuit analysis.
  • Power distribution networks.
  • Transformer and equivalent-circuit analysis.
  • Electronic amplifier circuits.
  • Analog and digital circuit design.
  • Solar PV and battery systems.
  • Electrical control systems.
  • Power converters and inverter circuits.
  • Network and communication circuits.

Common Mistakes When Applying KCL and KVL

1. Mixing Sign Conventions

One of the most common errors is changing the sign convention halfway through the calculation. Select a current or loop direction and maintain it consistently.

2. Forgetting Voltage Polarity

When applying KVL, carefully identify the polarity of each voltage source and the voltage drop across each component.

3. Incorrect Current Direction

The assumed current direction does not have to be correct initially. If the calculated current is negative, it simply means that the actual current flows opposite to the assumed direction.

4. Applying KCL to the Wrong Point

KCL is applied at a node or junction. Be careful not to treat an entire branch or loop as a single node unless the circuit topology justifies it.

5. Algebra Errors

Many circuit-analysis mistakes are actually algebraic mistakes. After obtaining a solution, substitute the values back into the original KCL or KVL equation to verify the result.

Key Takeaways

  • KCL states that the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero.
  • KVL states that the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.
  • KCL is based on conservation of electric charge.
  • KVL is based on conservation of energy.
  • Both laws are commonly used with Ohm's law.
  • KCL is fundamental to nodal analysis.
  • KVL is fundamental to mesh analysis.
  • Correct sign conventions are essential for obtaining the correct result.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Kirchhoff's Current Law?

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero. Equivalently, the total current entering a node equals the total current leaving the node.

What is Kirchhoff's Voltage Law?

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around a closed loop is zero.

What is the difference between KCL and KVL?

KCL applies to currents at a circuit node, while KVL applies to voltages around a closed circuit loop.

Can KCL and KVL be used together?

Yes. KCL and KVL are commonly used together with Ohm's law to solve complex electrical circuits using methods such as nodal analysis and mesh analysis.

What happens if a calculated current is negative?

A negative current normally means that the actual current flows in the opposite direction from the direction initially assumed during the analysis.
