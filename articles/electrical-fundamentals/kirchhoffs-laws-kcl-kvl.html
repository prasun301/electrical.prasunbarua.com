Kirchhoff's Laws: KCL and KVL Explained with Examples

Learn Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL), understand their connection to conservation laws, and apply them to electrical circuit analysis using practical worked examples.

By Prasun Barua • Published September 13, 2026 • Updated September 13, 2026 • 12 min read

Kirchhoff's Laws are fundamental principles used to analyze electrical circuits. They are especially useful when a circuit contains multiple branches, nodes, voltage sources or loops that cannot be analyzed conveniently using only Ohm's Law.

The two laws are:

  • Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — applies to currents at a circuit node or junction.
  • Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) — applies to voltage changes around a closed circuit loop.

KCL is based on the conservation of electric charge, while KVL is based on the conservation of energy. Together with Ohm's Law, they provide a foundation for systematic electrical circuit analysis.

Quick engineering answer: KCL states that the total current entering a node equals the total current leaving the node. KVL states that the algebraic sum of voltage changes around a closed loop is zero.

What Are Kirchhoff's Laws?

Kirchhoff's Laws were formulated by German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and became fundamental tools for electrical circuit analysis.

They are particularly useful for circuits that contain several branches and loops and cannot be reduced easily into simple series and parallel combinations.

Kirchhoff's first law is the junction rule, now commonly called KCL. It relates the currents entering and leaving a node.

Kirchhoff's second law is the loop rule, now commonly called KVL. It relates the voltage changes encountered while travelling around a closed path.

These rules are applications of two fundamental conservation principles:

  • Conservation of electric charge
  • Conservation of energy

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of all currents at a circuit node is zero.

∑ I = 0
Algebraic sum of currents at a node

Equivalently, the total current entering a node must equal the total current leaving the node.

∑ Iin = ∑ Iout
Total current entering = total current leaving

Physical basis of KCL

KCL follows from the conservation of electric charge. Charge cannot accumulate indefinitely at an ideal circuit node. Therefore, the amount of charge entering a junction must equal the amount leaving it.

KCL sign convention

A convenient convention is to consider currents entering the node as positive and currents leaving the node as negative.

For example, if two currents enter a node and two currents leave it:

I1 + I2 − I3 − I4 = 0

Therefore:

I1 + I2 = I3 + I4

KCL worked example

Example 1: Finding an unknown current

Suppose 5 A and 3 A enter a node, while 4 A and an unknown current I leave the node. Determine I.

5 + 3 = 4 + I
I = 8 − 4
I = 4 A

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage changes around any closed circuit loop is zero.

∑ V = 0
Algebraic sum of voltage changes around a closed loop

KVL is based on the conservation of energy. When a charge moves around a complete closed loop, the net change in electrical potential energy must be zero.

KVL sign convention

When applying KVL, choose a direction around the loop and keep the same direction throughout the calculation.

A common passive sign convention is:

  • Moving from negative to positive through a voltage source gives a voltage rise.
  • Moving from positive to negative through a voltage source gives a voltage drop.
  • Moving through a resistor in the direction of current gives a voltage drop of IR.
  • Moving through a resistor opposite to the current direction gives a voltage rise of IR.

KVL worked example

Example 2: Simple series circuit

Consider a circuit containing a 12 V source and two series resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω. Determine the circuit current.

First calculate the total resistance:

RT = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω

Using Ohm's Law:

I = V / R
I = 12 / 6 = 2 A

Applying KVL around the loop:

+12 − (2 × 2) − (2 × 4) = 0
12 − 4 − 8 = 0
0 = 0 ✓

Using KCL and KVL Together

Many practical electrical circuits require both Kirchhoff's Laws. KCL can establish relationships between branch currents, while KVL provides relationships between voltages around individual loops.

Ohm's Law is then used to relate voltage, resistance and current:

V = IR
Ohm's Law

The three principles can therefore be combined to solve complex electrical networks systematically.

Relationship to Mesh and Node Analysis

Kirchhoff's Laws form the theoretical foundation of several circuit-analysis techniques.

Node-voltage analysis

Node-voltage analysis primarily applies KCL to determine unknown node voltages. Ohm's Law is used to express branch currents in terms of node voltages.

Mesh-current analysis

Mesh analysis primarily applies KVL to determine unknown mesh currents in planar circuits.

These techniques are widely used in electrical engineering, electronics, power systems and control circuits.

Practical Applications of Kirchhoff's Laws

Kirchhoff's Laws are used in many areas of electrical and electronic engineering, including:

  • DC circuit analysis
  • AC circuit analysis
  • Electronic circuit design
  • Power-system analysis
  • Solar PV system analysis
  • Battery and energy-storage systems
  • Control systems
  • Electrical network modelling
  • Fault and troubleshooting analysis

Common Mistakes When Applying Kirchhoff's Laws

Several mistakes can lead to incorrect circuit equations. The most common include:

  • Changing the assumed current direction during the calculation.
  • Mixing voltage-rise and voltage-drop conventions.
  • Forgetting the polarity of voltage sources.
  • Applying KVL to a path that is not a closed loop.
  • Writing KCL without maintaining a consistent current sign convention.
  • Forgetting that a negative calculated current simply indicates the actual direction is opposite to the assumed direction.
Engineering tip: Choose current directions and loop directions arbitrarily at the beginning, but keep your assumptions and sign conventions consistent throughout the calculation.

Key Takeaways

  • KCL: The algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero.
  • KVL: The algebraic sum of voltage changes around a closed loop is zero.
  • KCL follows from conservation of electric charge.
  • KVL follows from conservation of energy.
  • Kirchhoff's Laws are often used together with Ohm's Law.
  • Negative calculated currents indicate that the actual current direction is opposite to the assumed reference direction.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)?

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of currents at a circuit node is zero. Equivalently, the total current entering a node equals the total current leaving the node.

What is Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)?

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage changes around any closed circuit loop is zero.

What is the difference between KCL and KVL?

KCL deals with current at circuit nodes and is based on conservation of charge. KVL deals with voltage around closed loops and is based on conservation of energy.

Can Kirchhoff's Laws be used with Ohm's Law?

Yes. Kirchhoff's Laws and Ohm's Law are commonly used together to determine unknown currents and voltages in electrical circuits.

What happens if a calculated Kirchhoff current is negative?

A negative current normally means that the actual current flows in the direction opposite to the assumed reference direction.
