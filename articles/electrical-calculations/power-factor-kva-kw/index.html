Electrical Power: kW, kVA, kVAR & Power Factor Explained

Understand the difference between real, apparent and reactive power, learn how kW, kVA, kVAR and power factor are related, and solve practical single-phase and three-phase electrical power calculations.

By Prasun Barua • Published September 13, 2026 • Updated September 13, 2026 • 10 min read

Electrical power in an AC system is commonly described using three related quantities: real power, apparent power and reactive power. These are expressed in kilowatts (kW), kilovolt-amperes (kVA) and kilovolt-amperes reactive (kVAR).

Understanding these quantities is important when sizing transformers, generators, cables, switchgear, inverters, capacitors and other electrical equipment. It is also essential for interpreting equipment nameplates and calculating current in AC systems.

Quick answer: kW is real power, kVA is apparent power, and kVAR is reactive power. Power factor connects real and apparent power: PF = kW ÷ kVA.

What Are kW, kVA and kVAR?

In AC power systems, voltage and current can be out of phase. Because of this phase relationship, the electrical power supplied to a load is not always converted entirely into useful work. The three power quantities help describe what is happening in the system.

kW — Real Power

kW (kilowatt) represents real, or active, power. It is the portion of electrical power that is converted into useful work, heat, light or mechanical output. Motors, heaters, lamps and electronic equipment ultimately consume real power to perform their intended functions.

For example, a motor may be rated to deliver mechanical output while drawing a corresponding amount of real electrical power from the supply.

kVA — Apparent Power

kVA (kilovolt-ampere) represents apparent power. It is the product of RMS voltage and RMS current, without initially considering the phase angle between them.

Equipment such as transformers, generators and many UPS systems are commonly rated in kVA because their current-carrying and thermal limits depend on voltage and current.

kVAR — Reactive Power

kVAR (kilovolt-ampere reactive) represents reactive power. Reactive power is associated with the periodic exchange of energy between the AC source and reactive elements such as inductors and capacitors.

Inductive loads such as motors and transformers commonly require positive reactive power, while capacitors can supply reactive power and are often used for power factor correction.

Real, Apparent and Reactive Power

The relationship between real power P, reactive power Q and apparent power S is often visualized using the power triangle.

S² = P² + Q²
Apparent power squared equals real power squared plus reactive power squared

Using engineering units:

  • P = real power, normally expressed in kW
  • Q = reactive power, normally expressed in kVAR
  • S = apparent power, normally expressed in kVA

These quantities form a right triangle for a sinusoidal AC system. The angle between apparent power and real power is commonly represented by φ, the phase angle between voltage and current.

PF = P ÷ S
Power factor equals real power divided by apparent power

What Is Power Factor?

Power factor (PF) indicates how effectively the apparent power drawn from an AC supply is associated with real power. For a sinusoidal system, power factor is the cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current.

PF = cos φ = kW ÷ kVA
For a sinusoidal AC system

A power factor of 1.0 means that real power equals apparent power. A lower power factor means that more current is required to deliver the same amount of real power.

Many industrial loads, especially induction motors and transformers, operate at a lagging power factor because current lags voltage. Capacitive compensation can be used to improve the overall power factor where appropriate.

Engineering note: Power factor is not simply a measure of whether equipment is efficient. It describes the relationship between real and apparent power. A device can have good energy efficiency while still requiring reactive power.

Electrical Power Formulas

The correct power equation depends on whether the circuit is DC, single-phase AC or three-phase AC. For AC systems, the power factor must be included when calculating real power.

DC or Purely Resistive Power

P = V × I
Real power for DC or a purely resistive AC load

Single-Phase AC

P = V × I × PF
Real power in a single-phase AC circuit
S = V × I
Apparent power in a single-phase AC circuit
Q = V × I × sin φ
Reactive power in a single-phase sinusoidal AC circuit

Three-Phase AC

P = √3 × VL × IL × PF
Real power for a balanced three-phase system
S = √3 × VL × IL
Apparent power for a balanced three-phase system
Q = √3 × VL × IL × sin φ
Reactive power for a balanced three-phase sinusoidal system

Here, VL is line-to-line RMS voltage and IL is line current. When using these equations, keep the voltage and current units consistent and convert watts to kilowatts by dividing by 1,000.

Converting Between kW, kVA and kVAR

kW = kVA × PF
Calculate real power from apparent power and power factor
kVA = kW ÷ PF
Calculate apparent power from real power and power factor
kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)
Calculate reactive power magnitude from apparent and real power

Single-Phase Power Calculation Example

Consider a single-phase load supplied at 230 V, drawing 10 A, with a power factor of 0.80. Calculate its apparent power and real power.

Given

  • Voltage = 230 V
  • Current = 10 A
  • Power factor = 0.80

First calculate apparent power:

S = V × I S = 230 × 10 S = 2,300 VA S = 2.30 kVA

Now calculate real power:

P = V × I × PF P = 230 × 10 × 0.80 P = 1,840 W P = 1.84 kW

Therefore, the load requires approximately 2.30 kVA of apparent power while consuming 1.84 kW of real power.

Three-Phase Power Calculation Example

Consider a balanced three-phase load supplied at 400 V line-to-line, drawing 20 A line current at a power factor of 0.90. Calculate the apparent power and real power.

Given

  • Line voltage = 400 V
  • Line current = 20 A
  • Power factor = 0.90

Apparent power:

S = √3 × VL × IL S = 1.732 × 400 × 20 S ≈ 13,856 VA S ≈ 13.86 kVA

Real power:

P = √3 × VL × IL × PF P = 1.732 × 400 × 20 × 0.90 P ≈ 12,471 W P ≈ 12.47 kW

The load therefore requires approximately 13.86 kVA of apparent power and consumes approximately 12.47 kW of real power.

Why Low Power Factor Matters

For a given real power and voltage, a lower power factor results in higher current. This is important because conductors, transformers, generators, switchgear and other equipment are affected by current and apparent power rather than real power alone.

Higher Current

From the three-phase power equation, current can be written as:

IL = P ÷ (√3 × VL × PF)
Three-phase line current for a given real power

If power factor decreases while real power and voltage remain unchanged, the required current increases.

Increased Electrical Losses

Conductor losses are approximately proportional to I²R. Therefore, higher current can produce higher resistive losses and greater voltage drop in conductors.

Equipment Loading

Transformers, generators and UPS systems are commonly limited by their apparent-power and current ratings. A low power factor can use more of the available kVA capacity to deliver the same amount of kW.

Important: A lower power factor does not automatically mean that the electrical equipment is wasting the same percentage of energy. It means that more apparent power and current are required relative to the real power being delivered.

Power Factor Correction

Power factor correction reduces the reactive power that must be supplied by the upstream electrical system. In industrial installations, capacitor banks are commonly used for suitable inductive loads. Active power-factor-correction circuits are also used in many electronic power supplies.

The objective is not to make every load operate at exactly unity power factor under every condition. The correction method and target should be selected according to the load profile, system characteristics, equipment ratings, utility requirements and applicable standards.

Example of kVAR Reduction

Suppose an installation consumes 100 kW at an initial power factor of 0.80. Its apparent power is:

S = kW ÷ PF S = 100 ÷ 0.80 S = 125 kVA

If the power factor is improved to 0.95 while real power remains 100 kW:

S = 100 ÷ 0.95 S ≈ 105.26 kVA

The apparent-power requirement falls from approximately 125 kVA to 105.26 kVA. This can reduce current and free capacity in upstream equipment, provided the correction system is correctly designed and controlled.

Apparent power Combined voltage-current power requirement of an AC system kVA kVA = kW ÷ PF
Reactive power Energy exchanged with reactive elements such as inductors and capacitors kVAR kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)

Where These Calculations Are Used

kW, kVA, kVAR and power factor calculations are used throughout electrical engineering and energy systems.

  • Transformer and generator sizing
  • UPS and inverter capacity assessment
  • Cable and feeder current calculations
  • Motor and industrial load analysis
  • Switchgear and protection-system design
  • Voltage-drop and conductor-loss calculations
  • Power factor correction and capacitor-bank sizing
  • Solar PV, battery and hybrid-system load assessment
  • Electrical energy audits and demand analysis

kW and kWh Are Not the Same

kW measures power—the rate at which energy is being used or delivered. kWh measures energy—the amount of energy used or delivered over time. For example, a 5 kW load operating continuously for 4 hours consumes 20 kWh of energy, assuming its power remains 5 kW throughout the period.

Power factor is also different from efficiency. Efficiency compares useful output energy or power with input energy or power, while power factor compares real power with apparent power in an AC system.

Important Engineering Considerations

The formulas in this article are useful for fundamental electrical calculations, but real installations can have additional considerations. Harmonic currents, unbalanced phases, nonlinear loads, variable-speed drives and distorted waveforms can affect the relationship between voltage, current and power factor.

For detailed design work, use measured electrical data, manufacturer information, applicable local regulations and relevant electrical standards. Do not select a transformer, generator, cable, inverter or capacitor bank from kW alone when the equipment is limited by kVA or current.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between kW and kVA?

kW measures real power that performs useful work, while kVA measures apparent power in an AC system. They are related by kW = kVA × power factor.

What is power factor?

Power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power: PF = kW ÷ kVA. For a sinusoidal AC load, it is also the cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current.

What is kVAR?

kVAR is kilovolt-ampere reactive power. It represents the reactive component of AC power associated with energy exchange between the source and reactive elements such as inductors and capacitors.

How do you calculate three-phase power?

For a balanced three-phase system, real power is P = √3 × VL × IL × PF and apparent power is S = √3 × VL × IL.

Why is a low power factor undesirable?

For the same real power, a lower power factor requires higher current. Higher current can increase conductor losses, voltage drop and equipment loading and can reduce available system capacity.

Summary

Understanding kW, kVA, kVAR and power factor is fundamental to AC electrical system analysis. The quantities describe different aspects of the same electrical system and should not be used interchangeably.

kW = kVA × PF

Remember the basic distinction: kW is real power, kVA is apparent power, and kVAR is reactive power. Power factor connects real and apparent power, while the power triangle relates real, reactive and apparent power.

These concepts provide a foundation for more advanced topics such as single-phase and three-phase power calculations, transformer sizing, generator selection, voltage-drop analysis, power factor correction and solar PV system design.
