kW vs kVA vs kVAR: Power Factor Explained

Understand the difference between real, apparent and reactive power, learn how kW, kVA, kVAR and power factor are related, and use practical formulas for AC electrical systems.

By Prasun Barua • Published September 13, 2026 • Updated September 13, 2026 • 9 min read

In an AC electrical system, kW, kVA and kVAR describe different aspects of electrical power. Understanding the difference is important when selecting transformers, generators, UPS systems, inverters, cables and other electrical equipment.

The relationship between these quantities is determined largely by power factor. Real power is expressed in kW, apparent power in kVA, and reactive power in kVAR.

Quick answer: kW is real power, kVA is apparent power, and kVAR is reactive power. Power factor connects real and apparent power: PF = kW ÷ kVA.

What Are kW, kVA and kVAR?

AC loads can draw current that is not perfectly in phase with the supply voltage. As a result, electrical power can be described using three related quantities: real power, apparent power and reactive power.

kW — Real Power

kW (kilowatt) is the unit of real power, also called active power. It represents the portion of electrical power that is converted into useful work, heat, light or mechanical output.

For example, a motor consumes real electrical power to produce mechanical output, while a heater converts electrical power primarily into heat.

kVA — Apparent Power

kVA (kilovolt-ampere) is the unit of apparent power. It represents the combined voltage-current requirement of an AC system without multiplying by power factor.

Apparent power is especially important for equipment that is limited by voltage, current and thermal capacity. This is why transformers, generators and many UPS systems are commonly rated in kVA rather than kW.

kVAR — Reactive Power

kVAR (kilovolt-ampere reactive) is the unit of reactive power. Reactive power is associated with the exchange of stored energy between the AC source and reactive elements such as inductors and capacitors.

Motors and transformers are common inductive loads that require reactive power. Capacitors can provide reactive power locally and are widely used for power factor correction.

The Power Triangle: kW, kVAR and kVA

The relationship between real power, reactive power and apparent power can be represented by the power triangle.

In the power triangle:

  • kW is the horizontal or real-power component.
  • kVAR is the reactive-power component.
  • kVA is the magnitude of apparent power.
  • φ is the phase angle between voltage and current for a sinusoidal system.

The same relationship can be written as:

S² = P² + Q²
General power-triangle relationship using S, P and Q.

Where P is real power, Q is reactive power and S is apparent power.

What Is Power Factor?

Power factor (PF) expresses the relationship between real power and apparent power in an AC system.

PF = kW ÷ kVA
Power factor equals real power divided by apparent power.

For a sinusoidal AC system, power factor can also be expressed as:

PF = cos φ
Power factor as the cosine of the voltage-current phase angle.

A power factor of 1.0 means the real power equals the apparent power. A lower power factor means that more apparent power and current are required to deliver the same amount of real power.

Induction motors, transformers and other inductive loads commonly operate at a lagging power factor. Capacitors can be used to compensate for part of the reactive power demand.

Engineering note: Power factor should not be confused with efficiency. Efficiency compares useful output with input power, while power factor describes the relationship between real and apparent power in an AC system.

kW, kVA and kVAR Formulas

The following equations are useful when converting between real, apparent and reactive power.

Calculate kW from kVA and Power Factor

kW = kVA × PF
Calculate real power when apparent power and power factor are known.

Calculate kVA from kW and Power Factor

kVA = kW ÷ PF
Calculate apparent power required to deliver a given real power.

Calculate kVAR from kW and kVA

kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)
Calculate the magnitude of reactive power from apparent and real power.

Calculate kVAR from kW and Power Factor

kVAR = kW × tan φ
Calculate reactive power when real power and phase angle are known.

For practical AC system analysis, these relationships help determine equipment loading, reactive power demand and the effect of power factor on system current.

Single-Phase kW and kVA Example

Consider a single-phase load supplied at 230 V, drawing 10 A, with a power factor of 0.80. Calculate its apparent power and real power.

Given

  • Voltage = 230 V
  • Current = 10 A
  • Power factor = 0.80

First calculate apparent power:

S = V × I
S = 230 × 10
S = 2,300 VA
S = 2.30 kVA

Now calculate real power:

P = V × I × PF
P = 230 × 10 × 0.80
P = 1,840 W
P = 1.84 kW

Therefore, the load requires approximately 2.30 kVA of apparent power while consuming 1.84 kW of real power.

The difference between the two values is explained by the load's power factor.

Three-Phase kW and kVA Example

Consider a balanced three-phase load supplied at 400 V line-to-line, drawing 20 A line current at a power factor of 0.90.

Given

  • Line voltage = 400 V
  • Line current = 20 A
  • Power factor = 0.90

Apparent power:

S = √3 × VL × IL
S = 1.732 × 400 × 20
S ≈ 13,856 VA
S ≈ 13.86 kVA

Real power:

P = √3 × VL × IL × PF
P = 1.732 × 400 × 20 × 0.90
P ≈ 12,471 W
P ≈ 12.47 kW

Therefore, the load requires approximately 13.86 kVA of apparent power and consumes approximately 12.47 kW of real power.

Why Low Power Factor Matters

A low power factor becomes important when electrical equipment is limited by current or apparent power. For the same real power and voltage, reducing power factor increases the current required from the supply.

Higher Current

For a balanced three-phase system:

IL = P ÷ (√3 × VL × PF)
Three-phase line current for a given real power and power factor.

Because power factor appears in the denominator, a lower power factor results in higher current when real power and voltage remain unchanged.

Example: 100 kW Load

Consider a 100 kW load operating at a power factor of 0.80:

kVA = kW ÷ PF
kVA = 100 ÷ 0.80
kVA = 125 kVA

If the same 100 kW load operates at a power factor of 0.95:

kVA = 100 ÷ 0.95
kVA ≈ 105.26 kVA

Improving the power factor from 0.80 to 0.95 reduces the apparent-power requirement from 125 kVA to approximately 105.26 kVA.

Higher Conductor Losses

Since resistive conductor losses are approximately proportional to I²R, higher current can result in greater electrical losses and voltage drop.

Reduced Equipment Capacity

Transformers, generators, UPS systems and switchgear are commonly constrained by current and kVA ratings. A low power factor can therefore consume more of the available equipment capacity for the same useful kW demand.

Important: A low power factor does not mean that the same percentage of electrical energy is automatically wasted. It means that more apparent power and current are required relative to the real power being delivered.

Power Factor Correction

Power factor correction reduces the reactive power that must be supplied by the upstream electrical system. In industrial installations, capacitor banks are commonly used to compensate for inductive reactive power.

Active power-factor-correction circuits are also widely used in electronic power supplies. The appropriate correction method depends on the type of load, operating conditions, harmonics, system characteristics and applicable requirements.

Power Factor Correction Example

Suppose an industrial installation consumes 100 kW at an initial power factor of 0.80.

Its apparent power is:

S = kW ÷ PF
S = 100 ÷ 0.80
S = 125 kVA

If the power factor is improved to 0.95 while real power remains 100 kW:

S = 100 ÷ 0.95
S ≈ 105.26 kVA

The apparent-power requirement therefore falls by approximately:

125 − 105.26 = 19.74 kVA

This can reduce current and release capacity in upstream equipment. However, capacitor banks and other correction systems should be properly sized and controlled rather than installed simply to achieve the highest possible power factor.

Where kW, kVA and kVAR Calculations Are Used

These quantities are used throughout electrical engineering, power-system design and energy management.

  • Transformer sizing
  • Generator sizing
  • UPS capacity selection
  • Inverter capacity assessment
  • Motor and industrial load analysis
  • Cable and feeder current calculations
  • Switchgear and electrical distribution design
  • Power factor correction
  • Capacitor-bank sizing
  • Electrical energy audits
  • Solar PV and battery-system load assessment
  • Demand and maximum-load analysis

Why Transformers Are Rated in kVA

Transformers are commonly rated in kVA because their thermal and electrical limits are closely related to voltage and current. The actual kW delivered by a transformer depends partly on the power factor of the connected load.

Why Generators Are Rated in kVA

Generator alternators are also commonly rated in kVA because their windings and electrical components are affected by apparent power and current. A generator's available kW output depends on its kVA rating and allowable power factor.

kW and kWh Are Different

kW measures power, while kWh measures energy. For example, a 5 kW load operating continuously for 4 hours consumes 20 kWh of energy.

This distinction is important when interpreting electricity bills. kW relates to instantaneous or demand power, while kWh represents accumulated energy consumption over time.

kW vs kVA vs kVAR Comparison

Comparison of real, apparent and reactive power
Quantity Meaning Unit Key Relationship
Real power Power converted into useful work, heat, light or mechanical output kW kW = kVA × PF
Apparent power Combined voltage-current power requirement of an AC system kVA kVA = kW ÷ PF
Reactive power Power associated with energy exchange involving reactive elements kVAR kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)
Power factor Relationship between real power and apparent power Dimensionless PF = kW ÷ kVA

Important Engineering Considerations

The formulas in this article are useful for fundamental AC power calculations, but real electrical installations can be more complex. Nonlinear loads, harmonics, unbalanced phases, variable-speed drives and distorted waveforms can affect the relationship between voltage, current and power factor.

In modern electrical systems, the measured value may also be described using terms such as true power factor or displacement power factor. These should not automatically be treated as identical when significant harmonic distortion is present.

For detailed engineering design, use measured electrical data, manufacturer information, applicable local regulations and relevant electrical standards. Equipment should not be selected from kW alone when its rating is limited by kVA or current.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between kW and kVA?

kW measures real power, while kVA measures apparent power. They are related by kW = kVA × power factor.

What is the difference between kW and kVAR?

kW represents real power that performs useful work, while kVAR represents reactive power associated with energy exchange between the AC source and reactive elements such as inductors and capacitors.

What is power factor?

Power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power: PF = kW ÷ kVA. For a sinusoidal AC system, it is also equal to the cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current.

How do you calculate kVA from kW?

Divide real power by power factor: kVA = kW ÷ PF. For example, a 100 kW load at 0.80 power factor requires 125 kVA.

What is kVAR used for?

kVAR describes reactive power in AC systems. It is commonly used when analyzing motors, transformers, capacitor banks and power factor correction systems.

Why does low power factor increase current?

For the same real power and voltage, a lower power factor requires higher current. This can increase conductor losses, voltage drop and loading of transformers, generators and other electrical equipment.

Why are transformers rated in kVA instead of kW?

Transformer thermal and electrical limits are closely related to voltage and current. Because apparent power represents the voltage-current requirement without depending on load power factor, transformers are commonly rated in kVA.

Summary

Understanding kW, kVA, kVAR and power factor is fundamental to AC electrical system analysis. Although these quantities are related, they describe different aspects of electrical power and should not be used interchangeably.

kW = kVA × PF

Remember the basic distinction: kW is real power, kVA is apparent power, and kVAR is reactive power.

The power triangle relates these quantities through:

kVA² = kW² + kVAR²

Power factor indicates how much of the apparent power is represented by real power. A lower power factor generally means higher current for the same useful power, which can affect electrical losses, voltage drop and equipment capacity.

These concepts are particularly important for transformer and generator sizing, motor analysis, UPS and inverter selection, power factor correction, electrical distribution design and solar PV system assessment.
