How to Calculate Electrical Power: Formulas, Examples & Practical Guide

Learn how to calculate electrical power in DC, single-phase and three-phase systems using practical formulas, power factor, worked examples and engineering applications.

By Prasun Barua • Published August 27, 2026 • Updated August 27, 2026 • 12 min read

Electrical power is one of the most important quantities used in electrical engineering. It describes the rate at which electrical energy is transferred, converted or consumed.

Engineers use power calculations when selecting electrical equipment, sizing cables, estimating generator capacity, evaluating motors, analysing solar PV systems and determining electrical energy consumption.

The correct power formula depends on the electrical system. A simple DC circuit uses a different expression from a single-phase or three-phase AC system.

Quick answer: For a simple DC or purely resistive circuit: P = V × I For a balanced three-phase AC system: P = √3 × V × I × PF

What Is Electrical Power?

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted.

Power is measured in watts (W). Larger electrical systems are commonly described in kilowatts (kW), megawatts (MW) or gigawatts (GW).

1 kW = 1,000 W
Kilowatt is a unit of electrical power.

Power is different from electrical energy. Power describes the rate of energy transfer, while energy represents the total amount transferred over a period of time.

For more information about power and energy, see: Electrical Energy Explained: kWh vs kW .

Basic Electrical Power Formula

The fundamental electrical power relationship is:

P = V × I
Power = Voltage × Current

Where:

  • P = Power in watts (W)
  • V = Voltage in volts (V)
  • I = Current in amperes (A)

This relationship forms the basis of many electrical power calculations.

How to Calculate DC Electrical Power

For a DC circuit, electrical power is calculated using voltage and current:

P = V × I

Worked Examples

DC Power Example

Suppose a 24 V DC load draws 5 A.

P = V × I P = 24 × 5 P = 120 W

Therefore, the load consumes or transfers 120 W of electrical power.

Power From Resistance

When a resistive load is known, Ohm's Law can be combined with the power equation.

P = I²R
P = V² ÷ R

These formulas are particularly useful for resistor calculations and resistive loads.

How to Calculate AC Electrical Power

AC power calculations require additional consideration because voltage and current can have a phase relationship.

For sinusoidal AC systems, the relationship between active power, apparent power and power factor is important.

P = S × PF
Active power = Apparent power × Power factor

The power factor is commonly represented by cosφ.

Single-Phase AC Power Calculation

For a single-phase AC load, active power can be calculated as:

P = V × I × cosφ
Single-phase active power

Where:

  • P = Active power in watts (W)
  • V = RMS voltage in volts (V)
  • I = RMS current in amperes (A)
  • cosφ = Power factor

Single-Phase Example

Consider a single-phase 230 V load drawing 10 A at a power factor of 0.8.

Given:
Voltage = 230 V
Current = 10 A
Power factor = 0.8

Calculation:
P = V × I × PF
P = 230 × 10 × 0.8
P = 1,840 W

Therefore, the active power is approximately 1.84 kW.

Three-Phase Electrical Power Calculation

For a balanced three-phase system using line-to-line voltage and line current, active power is commonly calculated as:

P = √3 × V × I × PF
Three-phase active power

Where:

  • P = Active power in watts
  • V = Line-to-line RMS voltage
  • I = Line current
  • PF = Power factor

Three-Phase Example

Consider a balanced three-phase 400 V system supplying a load drawing 20 A at a power factor of 0.9.

Given:
Line voltage = 400 V
Line current = 20 A
Power factor = 0.9

Calculation:
P = √3 × V × I × PF
P = 1.732 × 400 × 20 × 0.9
P ≈ 12,470 W

Therefore, the active power is approximately 12.47 kW.

What Is Power Factor?

Power factor describes the relationship between active power and apparent power in an AC system.

PF = P ÷ S
Power factor = Active power ÷ Apparent power

A power factor closer to 1 indicates that a larger proportion of apparent power is being converted into active power, under the usual engineering interpretation of these quantities.

Motors, transformers and other inductive loads often operate with lagging power factor. Capacitive equipment can produce leading power factor.

Active, Reactive and Apparent Power

AC electrical systems are commonly described using three related power quantities.

Main AC power quantities and their units
Quantity Symbol Unit Meaning
Active power P W or kW Useful power transferred to the load
Reactive power Q var or kvar Power associated with reactive energy exchange in AC systems
Apparent power S VA or kVA Product of RMS voltage and current for the relevant system
S = P ÷ PF

For a sinusoidal system, active, reactive and apparent power are also related by the power triangle:

S² = P² + Q²

Electrical Power Calculation for a Motor

Motor calculations require careful attention to whether the stated power refers to electrical input power or mechanical output power.

Suppose a three-phase motor operates from a 400 V supply, draws 15 A and has a power factor of 0.85.

P = √3 × V × I × PF P = 1.732 × 400 × 15 × 0.85 P ≈ 8,833 W P ≈ 8.83 kW

The calculated electrical active input power is approximately 8.83 kW.

The mechanical output power will generally be lower because motors have efficiency losses.

η = Pout ÷ Pin
Motor efficiency

Electrical Power Calculation in Solar PV Systems

Power calculations are also fundamental to solar PV engineering.

For example, if a PV inverter is operating at 500 V DC and drawing 20 A DC:

P = V × I P = 500 × 20 P = 10,000 W P = 10 kW

This represents the instantaneous DC power under the stated operating conditions.

Actual AC output can differ because of inverter efficiency, operating limits and other system conditions.

Electrical Power vs Electrical Energy: kW and kWh

One of the most common mistakes in electrical calculations is confusing power with energy.

Difference between electrical power and electrical energy
Quantity Unit Meaning
Power W / kW Rate of electrical energy transfer
Energy Wh / kWh Total electrical energy transferred over time
E(kWh) = P(kW) × t(h)
Electrical energy = Power × Time

For example, a 5 kW load operating for 4 hours consumes:

E = 5 × 4 E = 20 kWh

How Electrical Power Is Measured

Electrical engineers commonly measure voltage, current and power using instruments such as multimeters, clamp meters and power analysers.

A simple power calculation based on measured voltage and current can be useful for DC or resistive loads.

For AC systems, a dedicated power meter or power analyser can measure active power, reactive power, apparent power and power factor directly.

Common Electrical Power Calculation Mistakes

  • Using P = V × I for a non-resistive AC load without considering power factor.
  • Using the wrong voltage type in a three-phase calculation.
  • Confusing line-to-line voltage with line-to-neutral voltage.
  • Forgetting the √3 factor in the common balanced three-phase formula.
  • Confusing kW with kWh.
  • Treating motor mechanical output power as the same as electrical input power.
  • Ignoring power factor in AC load calculations.
  • Forgetting unit conversions between W and kW or VA and kVA.

Practical Electrical Power Calculation Procedure

A reliable power calculation can be organized into the following steps:

  1. Identify whether the system is DC, single-phase AC or three-phase AC.
  2. Determine the correct voltage value and measurement reference.
  3. Determine the load current.
  4. Determine the power factor when required.
  5. Select the appropriate power formula.
  6. Perform the calculation using consistent units.
  7. Convert watts to kilowatts when appropriate.
  8. Check the result against equipment ratings and operating conditions.

Quick Reference: Electrical Power Formulas

Common electrical power formulas
System Formula Main Parameters

Resistive load P = V × I Voltage, current
Single-phase AC P = V × I × PF RMS voltage, current, power factor
Three-phase AC P = √3 × V × I × PF Line voltage, line current, power factor

Summary

Electrical power represents the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or consumed.

For a simple DC or resistive circuit:

P = V × I

For a single-phase AC load:

P = V × I × PF

For a balanced three-phase AC system using line-to-line voltage:

P = √3 × V × I × PF

Selecting the correct formula requires identifying the system type, voltage reference, current and power factor.

These calculations are fundamental to electrical design, equipment selection, cable sizing, motor analysis, generator sizing and renewable-energy systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic electrical power formula?

The basic formula is P = V × I, where P is power in watts, V is voltage in volts and I is current in amperes.

How do you calculate single-phase AC power?

For a single-phase AC load, active power can be calculated using P = V × I × PF, where PF is the power factor.

How do you calculate three-phase power?

For a balanced three-phase system using line-to-line voltage, P = √3 × V × I × PF.

What is the difference between kW and kWh?

kW is a unit of power, while kWh is a unit of energy. Power describes the rate of energy transfer, while energy represents the total amount transferred over time.

What is power factor?

Power factor is the ratio of active power to apparent power in an AC electrical system. In a simplified sinusoidal system it is commonly expressed as cosφ.
