Electrical Calculations

How to Calculate Electrical Power: Formulas, Examples & Guide

Learn how to calculate electrical power in DC, single-phase and three-phase systems using practical formulas, worked examples, power factor and engineering applications.

By Prasun Barua Published August 27, 2026 Updated September 13, 2026

Electrical power is one of the most important quantities used in electrical engineering. It tells us how quickly electrical energy is being transferred or consumed by a circuit, machine or electrical system.

The correct power calculation depends on the type of electrical system. For simple DC circuits, power is commonly calculated from voltage and current. For AC systems, power factor must often be considered, while balanced three-phase systems use a different form of the power equation.

This guide explains the main electrical power formulas, shows practical examples and explains how the calculations are applied in electrical design, equipment selection, motors, solar PV systems and power-system engineering.

What Is Electrical Power?

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred, converted or consumed. It is measured in watts (W).

A higher electrical power means that electrical energy is being transferred or consumed at a higher rate.

In practical electrical systems, power may be expressed in watts (W), kilowatts (kW), megawatts (MW), volt-amperes (VA), kilovolt-amperes (kVA), or kilovolt-amperes reactive (kVAR), depending on the type of power being discussed.

Basic Electrical Power Formula

The basic electrical power relationship is:

P = V × I

Where:

  • P = electrical power in watts (W)
  • V = voltage in volts (V)
  • I = current in amperes (A)

For example, if a load operates at 230 V and draws 5 A:

P = 230 × 5

P = 1,150 W

Therefore, the load consumes 1.15 kW of electrical power.

How to Calculate DC Power

In a DC circuit, electrical power can normally be calculated directly from voltage and current:

P = V × I

When resistance is known, two additional forms of the power equation are useful:

P = I²R

P = V²/R

These equations are derived from Ohm's law and are particularly useful when voltage, current or resistance is the known quantity in a circuit.

For a detailed explanation of voltage, current and resistance relationships, see the Ohm's Law guide .

How to Calculate AC Power

AC power calculations require additional consideration because voltage and current can have a phase difference, particularly when the load contains inductive or capacitive components.

This phase relationship is represented by the power factor (PF).

For AC systems, it is useful to distinguish between real power, apparent power and reactive power.

Single-Phase AC Power Calculation

For a single-phase AC load, real power can be calculated using:

P = V × I × PF

Since power factor is commonly expressed as:

PF = cos φ

the equation may also be written as:

P = V × I × cos φ

Single-Phase Example

Consider a single-phase load operating at 230 V, drawing 10 A, with a power factor of 0.80.

P = V × I × PF

P = 230 × 10 × 0.80

P = 1,840 W

P = 1.84 kW

The corresponding apparent power is:

S = V × I

S = 230 × 10 = 2.30 kVA

Three-Phase AC Power Calculation

For a balanced three-phase AC system, real power can be calculated using:

P = √3 × VL × IL × PF

Where:

  • VL = line-to-line voltage
  • IL = line current
  • PF = power factor

Three-Phase Example

Consider a balanced three-phase load operating at 400 V line-to-line, drawing 20 A, with a power factor of 0.90.

P = √3 × VL × IL × PF

P = √3 × 400 × 20 × 0.90

P ≈ 12,470 W

P ≈ 12.47 kW

The apparent power is:

S = √3 × VL × IL

S = √3 × 400 × 20

S ≈ 13.86 kVA

Power Factor and Electrical Power

Power factor describes the relationship between real power and apparent power in an AC electrical system.

PF = P / S

A power factor of 1.0 means that real power and apparent power are equal. Lower power factor means that more apparent power is required to deliver the same amount of real power.

Engineering note: Power factor is especially important when sizing transformers, generators, UPS systems, cables, switchgear and other electrical equipment. For a detailed explanation of kW, kVA, kVAR and power-factor relationships, see kW vs kVA vs kVAR: Power Factor Explained .

Active Power, Apparent Power and Reactive Power

AC electrical systems commonly involve three related power quantities.

Quantity Symbol Unit Meaning
Real or active power P W / kW Power that performs useful work or is converted into heat, mechanical output, light and other forms of useful energy.
Apparent power S VA / kVA Combined electrical loading represented by voltage and current.
Reactive power Q VAR / kVAR Power associated with energy storage and return in inductive and capacitive components.

The relationship between these quantities can be expressed as:

S² = P² + Q²

S = P / PF

Electrical Power Calculation Examples

The following examples demonstrate how the formulas are applied to common electrical engineering situations.

Motor Power Calculation Example

Consider a three-phase motor operating at 400 V, drawing 15 A, with a power factor of 0.85.

P = √3 × V × I × PF

P = √3 × 400 × 15 × 0.85

P ≈ 8,833 W

P ≈ 8.83 kW

This is the electrical real input power based on the stated operating conditions. The mechanical output power of the motor will be lower after accounting for motor efficiency and other losses.

Solar PV Power Calculation Example

For a simple DC solar PV example, suppose a PV string or array is operating at 500 V and producing 20 A.

P = V × I

P = 500 × 20

P = 10,000 W = 10 kW

In an actual solar PV system, the operating power changes with irradiance, temperature, module characteristics, MPPT operation and other system conditions.

kW vs kWh

Power and energy are related but they are not the same quantity.

Quantity Unit Meaning
Power kW Rate of electrical energy transfer or consumption.
Energy kWh Electrical energy consumed or generated over time.

For example, a 5 kW load operating continuously for 4 hours consumes:

Energy = Power × Time

Energy = 5 × 4 = 20 kWh

Measuring Electrical Power

Electrical power can be measured directly using suitable electrical measurement instruments.

Common instruments include:

  • Digital power meters
  • Power-quality analyzers
  • Energy meters
  • Clamp meters with power measurement functions
  • Multifunction electrical meters

For three-phase systems, the measurement equipment and connection method should be appropriate for the system configuration and voltage level.

Common Electrical Power Calculation Mistakes

Several common mistakes can lead to incorrect power calculations.

  • Forgetting power factor when calculating real AC power.
  • Using line-to-neutral voltage when a three-phase line-to-line voltage is required.
  • Confusing kW with kWh.
  • Confusing real power with apparent power.
  • Using the three-phase equation for a single-phase circuit.
  • Ignoring equipment efficiency when estimating useful mechanical output.
  • Mixing watts and kilowatts without converting units.

Practical Electrical Power Calculation Procedure

A practical engineering workflow can be summarized as follows:

  1. Identify whether the system is DC, single-phase AC or three-phase AC.
  2. Determine the operating voltage.
  3. Determine the operating current or load rating.
  4. Determine the power factor for AC loads when required.
  5. Select the appropriate power formula.
  6. Perform the calculation using consistent units.
  7. Convert the result into W, kW, VA or kVA as appropriate.
  8. Consider efficiency, demand, operating conditions and design margin when using the result for equipment selection.

Electrical Power Formula Quick Reference

System Formula Main Parameters
DC P = V × I Voltage and current
Resistive load P = I²R Current and resistance
Resistive load P = V²/R Voltage and resistance
Single-phase AC P = V × I × PF Voltage, current and power factor
Three-phase AC P = √3 × VL × IL × PF Line voltage, line current and power factor

Summary

Electrical power calculation begins with identifying the electrical system and selecting the correct formula.

  • DC power: P = V × I
  • Resistive power: P = I²R or P = V²/R
  • Single-phase AC real power: P = V × I × PF
  • Three-phase AC real power: P = √3 × VL × IL × PF

For practical electrical design, power factor, equipment efficiency, system voltage, operating current and the distinction between kW, kVA, kVAR and kWh should all be considered appropriately.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic electrical power formula?

The basic electrical power formula is P = V × I, where P is power in watts, V is voltage in volts and I is current in amperes.

How do you calculate single-phase AC power?

Single-phase AC real power can be calculated using P = V × I × PF, where V is voltage, I is current and PF is power factor.

How do you calculate three-phase power?

For a balanced three-phase system, real power can be calculated using P = √3 × VL × IL × PF.

What is the difference between kW, kVA and kVAR?

kW represents real power, kVA represents apparent power and kVAR represents reactive power. Their relationship is explained using the power triangle and power factor.

See the dedicated guide: kW vs kVA vs kVAR: Power Factor Explained .

Why is power factor important?

Power factor is important because a lower power factor generally requires higher current to deliver the same real power. This can increase electrical equipment loading, losses and voltage-drop considerations.
