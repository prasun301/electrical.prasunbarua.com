How to Calculate Electrical Power: Formulas, Examples & Practical Guide

Learn how to calculate electrical power in DC, single-phase and three-phase systems using practical formulas, power factor, worked examples and engineering applications.

By Prasun Barua • Published August 27, 2026 • Updated August 27, 2026 • 12 min read

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or consumed by an electrical circuit or device.

Power calculation is fundamental to electrical engineering because it is used for equipment selection, cable sizing, protection, load assessment, transformer sizing, generator selection, solar PV system design and energy analysis.

Quick engineering answer: For a basic DC or resistive circuit: P = V × I For a balanced three-phase AC system: P = √3 × V × I × PF where PF is the power factor.

What Is Electrical Power?

Electrical power describes how quickly electrical energy is transferred from a source to a load. It is normally measured in watts (W), kilowatts (kW) or megawatts (MW).

The correct power equation depends on the type of electrical system and the information available, such as voltage, current, resistance, power factor and phase configuration.

Basic Electrical Power Formula

The most basic electrical power relationship is:

P = V × I
Electrical power = Voltage × Current

Where:

  • P = Power in watts (W)
  • V = Voltage in volts (V)
  • I = Current in amperes (A)

DC Electrical Power

In a DC circuit, electrical power can be calculated directly from voltage and current:

P = V × I

For example, if a DC circuit operates at 24 V and draws 5 A:

P = V × I
P = 24 × 5
P = 120 W

Therefore, the electrical load consumes 120 W.

DC Power Using Resistance

Using Ohm's Law, electrical power can also be expressed as:

P = I²R

Or:

P = V² ÷ R

AC Electrical Power

In AC systems, power calculation depends on the relationship between voltage and current. For practical AC loads, power factor must often be considered.

P = S × PF
Active power = Apparent power × Power factor

Here:

  • P = Active power in watts (W)
  • S = Apparent power in volt-amperes (VA)
  • PF = Power factor

Single-Phase AC Power

For a single-phase AC load, active power can be calculated using:

P = V × I × cosφ
Single-phase active power

Where:

  • V = RMS voltage
  • I = RMS current
  • cosφ = Power factor

Single-Phase Example

Consider a 230 V single-phase load drawing 10 A at a power factor of 0.8.

P = V × I × PF
P = 230 × 10 × 0.8
P = 1840 W
P = 1.84 kW

Therefore, the load consumes approximately 1.84 kW of active power.

Three-Phase AC Power

For a balanced three-phase AC system using line-to-line voltage, active power is commonly calculated using:

P = √3 × V × I × PF
Three-phase active power

Where:

  • V = Line-to-line voltage
  • I = Line current
  • PF = Power factor

Three-Phase Example

Consider a balanced three-phase load operating at 400 V, drawing 20 A at a power factor of 0.9.

P = √3 × V × I × PF
P = 1.732 × 400 × 20 × 0.9
P ≈ 12,470 W
P ≈ 12.47 kW

Therefore, the active power is approximately 12.47 kW.

Power Factor

Power factor represents the relationship between active power and apparent power in an AC system.

PF = P ÷ S

A lower power factor means that more apparent power is required to deliver the same amount of active power.

Active, Reactive and Apparent Power

Quantity Symbol Unit Description
Active power P W / kW Useful or real power consumed by the load
Reactive power Q var / kvar Power associated with reactive energy exchange
Apparent power S VA / kVA Combined apparent electrical loading

Apparent power can be related to active power and power factor by:

S = P ÷ PF

For an ideal sinusoidal system:

S² = P² + Q²

Worked Examples

Motor Power Calculation Example

Consider a three-phase motor operating at 400 V, drawing 15 A with a power factor of 0.85.

P = √3 × V × I × PF
P = 1.732 × 400 × 15 × 0.85
P ≈ 8,833 W
P ≈ 8.83 kW

The motor input active power is approximately 8.83 kW.

If the motor efficiency is 90%, the approximate mechanical output power is:

η = Pout ÷ Pin
Pout = 8.83 × 0.90
Pout ≈ 7.95 kW

Solar PV Power Calculation Example

For a DC solar PV string operating at 500 V and producing 20 A:

P = V × I
P = 500 × 20
P = 10,000 W
P = 10 kW

Therefore, the instantaneous DC electrical power is approximately 10 kW.

kW vs kWh: Power and Energy

Power and energy are related but represent different quantities.

Quantity Unit Meaning
Power kW Rate of energy transfer
Energy kWh Amount of energy consumed or produced

Energy can be calculated from power and operating time:

E = P × t
Energy = Power × Time

For example, a 5 kW load operating for 4 hours consumes:

E = 5 × 4
E = 20 kWh

Measuring Electrical Power

Electrical power can be measured or determined using different instruments depending on the application.

  • Wattmeter: directly measures electrical power.
  • Power analyzer: measures voltage, current, power factor, active power, reactive power and other electrical parameters.
  • Multimeter and clamp meter: can be used to measure voltage and current, after which power can be calculated.
  • Smart meter: can provide electrical energy and, depending on the device, power and other electrical parameters.

Common Electrical Power Calculation Mistakes

Some common mistakes in electrical power calculations include:

  • Using line voltage incorrectly in a three-phase formula
  • Ignoring power factor in AC calculations
  • Confusing kW with kWh
  • Mixing RMS and peak values
  • Using phase voltage where line-to-line voltage is required
  • Ignoring system efficiency when calculating useful output power
  • Using a DC formula directly for an AC load without considering power factor

Practical Electrical Power Calculation Procedure

A practical engineering calculation can generally follow these steps:

  1. Identify whether the system is DC, single-phase AC or three-phase AC.
  2. Determine the system voltage.
  3. Determine the load current.
  4. Determine the power factor for AC loads where applicable.
  5. Select the appropriate power equation.
  6. Calculate active, reactive or apparent power as required.
  7. Apply equipment efficiency where output power is required.
  8. Convert the result into W, kW, VA, kVA, var or kvar as appropriate.
  9. Compare the calculated result with equipment ratings and design requirements.

Electrical power is the rate of electrical energy transfer. The appropriate calculation depends on whether the system is DC, single-phase AC or three-phase AC.

P = V × I
Basic DC or resistive power
P = V × I × PF
Single-phase AC active power

Balanced three-phase AC active power

Power factor, efficiency and system configuration should be considered when performing practical electrical engineering calculations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic electrical power formula?

The basic electrical power formula is P = V × I, where P is power in watts, V is voltage in volts and I is current in amperes.

How do you calculate single-phase AC power?

For a single-phase AC load, active power can be calculated using P = V × I × cosφ, where V is voltage, I is current and cosφ is the power factor.

How do you calculate three-phase power?

For a balanced three-phase system using line-to-line voltage, active power can be calculated using P = √3 × V × I × PF.

What is the difference between kW and kWh?

kW is a unit of power, while kWh is a unit of energy. Power describes the rate of energy transfer, whereas energy represents the amount transferred over time.

What is power factor?

Power factor is the ratio of active power to apparent power in an AC electrical system. In a simplified sinusoidal system, it is commonly represented by cosφ.
